---- GUIDE 1: Best Indoor Plants for Indian Homes (published 12 January 2026, 6 min read, by the Growman team) ----
Summary: How to choose indoor plants that cope with Indian light, heat and humidity, and where to place them in a flat or house.

Start with the light you actually have.
The most common reason an indoor plant fails is not neglect; it is being placed somewhere it was never going to survive. Before buying, spend a day noticing how light moves through the room. A spot two metres back from a south- or west-facing window in most Indian cities receives bright indirect light for several hours, which suits the widest range of foliage plants. A north-facing room, an internal corridor, or a bathroom with a small high window is a genuinely low-light position, and only a narrow set of plants will hold their leaves there. Light also changes with the season: a balcony door that gives gentle morning sun in December can deliver harsh direct heat by April. If a plant scorches or pales every summer, the fix is usually to move it a metre further from the glass rather than to water it more.

Match the plant to the room.
- Bright indirect light, away from direct sun: most foliage plants do well; this is where a feature plant earns its place.
- Low light (north-facing rooms, corridors): choose plants specifically sold as low-light tolerant and expect slower growth rather than none.
- Air-conditioned rooms: air conditioning dries the air considerably. Plants here need less frequent but more thorough watering and should be kept out of the direct airflow.

Give new plants two weeks to settle.
A plant that has just moved from a nursery to a living room is adjusting to different light, humidity and airflow all at once. Losing a leaf or two in the first fortnight is normal. During that time keep it in its intended position, water only when the top few centimetres of soil are dry, and do not repot or fertilise. Repotting a plant that is already stressed is the fastest way to lose it. After a month, if the plant is pushing out new growth it has accepted the position. If it is still dropping leaves, change the light before changing anything else.

Plan for the summer and the monsoon.
Indian indoor growing has two demanding seasons. In the hot, dry pre-monsoon months pots dry out far faster and most plants need checking every two or three days. Once the monsoon arrives humidity climbs and the same pot may stay damp for a week; continuing a summer watering routine into the monsoon is one of the most reliable ways to cause root rot. Water by checking the soil, not by the calendar: push a finger two to three centimetres into the pot; if soil clings to the finger, wait.

---- GUIDE 2: How Often to Water Indoor Plants (published 20 January 2026, 5 min read, by the Growman team) ----
Summary: A practical watering method for Indian conditions: how to check the soil, how the seasons change the routine, and how to spot over- and under-watering.

Stop watering on a schedule.
Fixed routines (every Sunday, twice a week) fail because they ignore what actually determines how fast a pot dries: pot size and material, soil mix, room temperature, humidity and how much light the plant gets. The same plant may need water twice a week in May and once a fortnight in August. Replace the schedule with a check: push a finger two to three centimetres into the soil, or lift the pot and note its weight. Dry soil at that depth, or a noticeably light pot, means water. Damp soil means wait and check again in two days.

Water thoroughly, then let it drain.
Add water slowly across the whole surface until it runs out of the drainage hole, which wets the whole root ball. Small daily splashes encourage roots to stay near the surface where they dry out fastest. Then remove the runoff: a pot left standing in a saucer of water keeps the lower roots waterlogged and starved of air, and that, not dryness, is what kills most indoor plants. Tip the saucer out ten minutes after watering.

Read the symptoms correctly.
- Under-watered: leaves go limp but stay dry and papery, edges turn crisp and brown, and the soil pulls away from the sides of the pot.
- Over-watered: lower leaves turn yellow and drop while still soft, the soil stays wet for days, and the base of the stem may feel soft or smell sour.
- Both can cause drooping. Check the soil before deciding; drooping in wet soil means the roots are struggling and more water will finish them off.

Adjust for the pot and the season.
Terracotta breathes and dries faster than plastic or ceramic, so check terracotta pots more often. Large pots hold moisture far longer than small ones, so a newly potted-up plant often needs less frequent watering than before. In the monsoon, reduce watering markedly and improve airflow around plants. In the hot months, check more often and water early in the morning or after sunset so less is lost to evaporation.

---- GUIDE 3: Outdoor and Balcony Plant Care in India (published 3 February 2026, 6 min read, by the Growman team) ----
Summary: Caring for outdoor and balcony plants through Indian summers and monsoons: sun exposure, watering, wind, drainage and seasonal repotting.

Work out your real sun exposure.
Balconies vary enormously. A west-facing balcony above the third floor can take six hours of hard afternoon sun plus reflected heat from the building, which is a genuinely harsh position. An east-facing balcony gets gentler morning light and is far more forgiving. Watch where the shadow line falls at 11 AM and again at 4 PM to know what you can grow. Full-sun outdoor plants need that exposure to grow well and become sparse and leggy in shade. Conversely, moving a plant grown under nursery shade netting straight into full afternoon sun will scorch its leaves; introduce it over a week or two, increasing exposure gradually.

Drainage matters more outdoors.
Outdoor pots catch the full monsoon. A pot without a clear drainage path holds water for days during heavy rain and few plants tolerate that. Check that every drainage hole is open and not sitting flush against the floor; raising pots on feet or a couple of tiles makes a real difference. During extended heavy rain, move smaller pots under cover if possible. It is far easier to water a sheltered plant than to recover one whose roots have sat in water for a week.

Account for wind and heat.
Wind is the factor most people overlook on higher floors. It dries soil and leaves faster than sun alone and can snap tall stems. Group pots together so they shelter each other, place heavier pots on the exposed side, and stake anything tall before the pre-monsoon winds arrive rather than after. In peak summer a layer of mulch on the soil surface noticeably slows evaporation and keeps root temperatures down. Watering early in the morning gives the plant the whole day to use the water.

Repot and prune at the right time.
The best time to repot or hard-prune most outdoor plants is at the start of a growing season when the plant has the vigour to recover, typically as the weather turns after summer or early in the monsoon. Repotting in the middle of a heatwave asks a plant to rebuild its roots at exactly the moment it is under the most stress. Signs a plant is ready for a bigger pot: roots emerging from the drainage holes, water running straight through without wetting the soil, and growth slowing despite good conditions. Increase the pot diameter by a few centimetres rather than moving to something much larger.

SECTION 5: CATEGORY DESCRIPTIONS (AS STATED ON GROWMAN)
=====================================================

Indoor Plants (15 plants)
Indoor plants bring greenery into rooms, offices and balconies that get little or no direct sun. The main thing to get right is light: most foliage plants want bright but indirect light, while a smaller group genuinely tolerates a north-facing room or a dim corner. Choose based on the spot you have in mind, and remember that pots indoors dry out far more slowly during the monsoon than through summer.

Outdoor Plants (23 plants)
Outdoor plants are grown for terraces, gardens, balconies and boundaries where they get several hours of direct sun. Check your real sun exposure before choosing; a west-facing balcony above the third floor is a much harsher position than an east-facing one. Good drainage matters more outdoors than anywhere else, because pots here take the full monsoon.

Avenue (7 plants)
Avenue plants are the tall, columnar and canopy species used along driveways, boundaries, roads and large landscapes. They are selected for height, uniform shape and low maintenance once established, and are usually planted directly into the ground rather than kept in pots.

Fruit Plants (3 plants)
Fruit plants can be grown in pots on a terrace or balcony as well as in the ground. They need more sun, more root space and more consistent feeding than ornamental plants, so pick a position that gets direct light for most of the day and plan to move up a pot size as the plant establishes.

Medicinal (6 plants)
Medicinal and herbal plants are grown for their leaves, roots and traditional household uses. Most are hardy, tolerate containers well and prefer a sunny spot with free-draining soil. Consult a qualified practitioner before using any plant medicinally.

Ornamental Plants (6 plants)
Ornamental plants are chosen for their foliage, form and structure rather than their flowers: variegated leaves, sculpted shapes and plants that hold their look through the year. They work well as feature plants in entrances, living rooms and landscaped beds.

SECTION 14: SEASONAL CALENDAR FOR PLANT CARE IN INDIA
=====================================================
General pattern for most of peninsular and central India (Hyderabad-type climate). Local weather varies.

Summer / pre-monsoon (roughly March to early June):
- Hot and dry. Indoor and outdoor pots dry out fast; check soil every two to three days.
- Water early in the morning or after sunset. Mulch the soil surface to slow evaporation.
- Protect plants that came from nursery shade netting from sudden full afternoon sun. Group pots so they shade each other.
- Do not repot or hard-prune during a heatwave.
- Flowering trees: Amaltas (golden yellow), Gulmohar (red-orange), Cassia javanica (pink) and Pride of India (pink-purple) bloom in this period or just after.
- Stake tall plants before pre-monsoon winds arrive.

Monsoon (roughly June to September):
- Humidity high; pots stay damp. Reduce watering markedly and improve airflow.
- Make sure drainage holes are open and pots are raised off the floor. Shelter smaller pots during extended heavy rain.
- Watch for fungal disease, root rot, slugs and snails. Early monsoon is a good time to repot or prune many outdoor plants and to plant trees in the ground.

Post-monsoon / early autumn (roughly October to November, i.e. the current season at the time of this snapshot):
- A good time to repot, prune and plant out, because plants are at the start of a growing season and recover well.
- Sweet Autumn Clematis flowers in late summer to autumn.

Winter (roughly December to February):
- Cooler nights; growth slows. Water less. Protect frost- and cold-sensitive plants (Alocasia, Tibouchina, palms, dragon fruit) from cold draughts or exposure on very cold nights.
- Bright winter sun is gentle and many indoor plants can sit closer to the window.

SECTION 15: TROUBLESHOOTING PLANT PROBLEMS
=====================================================
Yellow lower leaves, soft stems, soil staying wet: overwatering or poor drainage. Let soil dry, empty the saucer, improve drainage and airflow. Reduce watering in the monsoon.

Brown, crispy leaf edges; limp dry leaves; soil pulling from pot edge: underwatering or very dry air. Water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage hole and check more often in summer.

Drooping in wet soil: roots are struggling from too much water; do not add more water.

Leaf drop right after buying or moving a plant: normal adjustment to new light, humidity and airflow. Leave it in one position for two to four weeks; do not repot or fertilise.

Scorched or bleached patches on leaves: too much direct sun. Move the plant a metre from the glass or give afternoon shade; introduce full sun gradually.

Leggy, sparse growth and pale leaves: not enough light. Move to a brighter spot (for a sun-loving outdoor plant, to a spot with 5-6 hours of direct sun).

Brown tips on palms and dracaenas: dry air, salts or fluoride in the water, or irregular watering; flush the pot occasionally and keep plants away from AC airflow.

Plant not flowering (hibiscus, jasmine, ixora, passiflora): usually not enough direct sun or too much nitrogen; give 5-6 hours of sun and use a flower-friendly fertiliser.

Yellow leaves with green veins on Ixora and other acid-loving plants: iron deficiency or alkaline soil; use an acidifying or chelated-iron feed.

Tiny insects on buds or new growth: aphids, mealybugs or whitefly; wash off with a strong water spray or treat early with a mild soap solution or neem-based spray.

Roots coming out of drainage holes, water running straight through, growth slowing: ready for a pot only a few centimetres wider; repot at the start of a growing season, not in a heatwave.

Q: Can I grow a fruit plant in a pot on a terrace?
A: Yes. Pomegranate, Dragon Fruit and Grape can be grown in large pots with direct sun for most of the day, regular feeding, and a pot upgrade as the plant grows. Grape and Dragon Fruit need a trellis or sturdy post.

Q: How often should I water indoor plants?
A: Not on a fixed schedule. Push a finger 2-3 cm into the soil; water only if it is dry, then water thoroughly until it drains and empty the saucer after ten minutes. Water far less in the monsoon and more often in peak summer.

Q: How long does a new plant take to settle in?
A: About two weeks to a month. Losing a leaf or two is normal. Do not repot or fertilise during that period.

Q: When is the best time to repot outdoor plants in India?
A: At the start of a growing season: as the weather turns after summer, or early in the monsoon. Avoid repotting during a heatwave.

Q: Is Ashoka tree (Polyalthia longifolia) the same as the sacred Ashoka?
